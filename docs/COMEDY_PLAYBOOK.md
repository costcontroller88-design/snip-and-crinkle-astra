# Comedy playbook: how Snip & Crinkle get laughs (research 8 Oct 2026)

Why this exists: comedy scored 5, 5, 4 in every Episode 11 round (mistake M16). The "gags" in those plans were cute
moments, not jokes. This file is the standard for the story-designer (gag design), the animator (comic timing), the
safety-checker (comedy safety) and the Lead. The blind reviewer carries its own short version. Sources are at the end.

## 1. Why ages 2-6 laugh (the science in five lines)
1. **Surprise that is safe.** Laughter comes when something unexpected happens and the child judges it harmless
   (Rothbart's arousal-safety model; McGraw's "benign violation"). Not surprising means no laugh. Not safe means fear, not a laugh.
2. **Wrong things are funny at this age.** From about 18 months children laugh at objects used the wrong way, and then at
   wrong names and wrong features (McGhee's stages). Two- to three-year-olds' own jokes are object misuse, mislabelling,
   category errors (a pig that goes "moo"), funny body actions, chasing and peekaboo (Hoicka & Akhtar 2012).
3. **Changed features win.** Preschoolers (3-7) laughed most at changed attributes, like a fish with big ears, and at
   "accidents" caused on purpose by someone else (Havigerová 2015). We use the first. The second is a prank and is ruled out (section 5).
4. **Laughter tells them it's a joke.** Toddlers (25-36 months) treat an odd action as a joke when the person laughs, and as a
   mistake when the person says "whoops" (Hoicka & Gattis 2008). A gag needs a laugh or grin signal. The lesson's
   mistake needs the opposite signal (an "oops" face, no laugh).
5. **Children laugh with company.** Preschoolers watching a wordless slapstick cartoon laughed about 8 times more with
   others than alone (Addyman et al. 2018). Being stuck in a bin got big laughs. Characters laughing *together* on
   screen give the viewer that company.

## 2. Gag types that work for 2-6 (use these; name the type in the plan)
| # | Type | What it is | Our example |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | **Wrong use** | an object used as something else (Carroll's "mimed metaphor") | Crinkle's watering can as a trumpet; a leaf as Pip's umbrella |
| G2 | **Wrong feature** | something gets a feature it shouldn't have | Crinkle's blue paint nose; Snip's ears drooping like noodles |
| G3 | **Last to notice** | the viewer sees what the character doesn't (dramatic irony) | something lands on Crinkle; everyone sees it first; he laughs hardest |
| G4 | **Copycat breaks** | the rule of three: two copies set the pattern, the third goes wrong or bigger | Pip copies the dance twice, then the third copy tips her into a roll |
| G5 | **Too far** | escalation: each try bigger, until it tips over | the wiggle-dance swings him round; a stack that grows one too many |
| G6 | **Funny body** | a benign body predicament: stuck, tangled, a soft sit-down, a sneeze | Snip stuck in a jumper; ears popping a helmet off |
| G7 | **Peekaboo / reveal** | hide and reveal; a cut shows the earlier shot meant something else (Carroll's "switch image") | Pip "hides" behind a stick much thinner than her |
| G8 | **Fooled sense** | a sound or sight is mistaken for something else, and the reveal makes it harmless | the scary shadow is Pip's own tuft |
| G9 | **Playful chase** | a chase or tickle everyone enjoys (play that only pretends to be rough) | the paw-bump that turns into a tickle |

**Not for us:** taboo or "gross" jokes (hard rule 3); pranks on someone (even when the child audience laughs);
anyone laughed at for being sad, unsure or wrong. **Not a gag (M16):** a smile, a cute wiggle, a copy with no
break, someone being sweet. That is charm. It is good, but it doesn't count toward the gag map.

## 3. The gag test: every gag in a plan passes all seven
| Part | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Trait | comes from a trait or running joke in `docs/CHARACTER_BIBLE.md` | Disney: gags from personality, not "clothesline" gags |
| 2 Set-up | the viewer can predict what *should* happen: ≥1.5 s on screen, planted earlier if it's a callback | surprise needs an expectation (Williams: anticipation is expectation) |
| 3 Pause | 0.3-0.6 s of stillness before the break; the music rests or holds | the "wait for it" beat; a sound before the break gives the joke away |
| 4 Break | what happens instead: ≥0.8 s, the key thing ≥1/10 of the frame or in close-up | the blocking rule; a hidden break is no gag |
| 5 Joke signal | within 1 s the one it happened to grins or laughs; others laugh *with* them | toddlers read laughter as "joke" (Hoicka & Gattis); company multiplies laughs |
| 6 Reaction | a face close-up ≥1.0 s (brows, ears, tuft carry it, like Gromit's brows) | the reaction is where the audience laughs |
| 7 Benign | nobody hurt; nothing precious broken; nothing a child could copy unsafely (section 5) | safe surprise = laughter; unsafe = fear or a copied risk |
Optional **topper:** one more beat that pushes it one step further (Pip copies the sit-down). Use at least one per episode.

## 4. Structure rules
- **Density:** a landed gag (all seven parts) every 15-20 s. Shorts: one by 5 s, then every 10-15 s.
- **Per episode:** one rule-of-three build (G4 or G5), one running-joke callback from the bible, one topper, and
  the ending's wow followed by a last small gag (the callback is ideal).
- **Mix:** at least 3 different gag types per episode; never the same type twice in a row.
- **Lesson and gags stay apart:** no gag within 2 s of the mistake or on the sad character; the lesson's mistake gets
  the "oops" signal. Gags come before the problem (to win the viewer) and after the fix (relief laughter); between
  problem and fix, gags come from the helper's trait.
- **Transformation Short:** the change itself should be a G2 (wrong feature) or G5 (too far) at its peak, with a reaction.
- **Act it out:** Aardman's directors performed gags for the animators. The designer writes each gag's timing as
  set-up, pause, break and reaction seconds, so the animator can stage it once.

## 5. Comedy safety (the safety-checker's checklist)
- Slapstick is the cartoon genre children most imitate, and TV injuries are mostly shown with no consequence and no
  reaction (Potts & Henderson; Wilson et al. via review). So: falls only from standing, onto a flat floor, a soft
  sit-down with a reaction; never from furniture or a height; no climbing; no hitting, pushing or throwing things at
  anyone; no "superhero" jumps; no pain faces played for laughs.
- No pranks, no taking, no tricking anyone (rule 3). A chase or tickle only if both laugh.
- Sound: no falling "wah-wah" or sad-trombone sound on someone's mistake; no other character laughing at the one
  who erred (gate 1 r1, R2-1, R2-3). Laughing at yourself is fine and models resilience.
- Nothing gross (taboo humour is real for 2-year-olds, but it is off our channel).

## 6. Comic timing card for the animator (30 fps, drawings on twos: 1 drawing = 0.067 s)
Adapted from Richard Williams, *The Animator's Survival Kit* (anticipation, takes and accents chapters). Slowed a
little for 2-6-year-olds.
| Move | Timing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Anticipation → action → reaction | anticipation slower than the action; the action can be 2-4 drawings; the reaction held | Tytla's three things; Chaplin: show what you'll do, do it, show you've done it |
| **Take** (surprise) | sees it (0.2 s) → squash down (0.2-0.3 s) → snap up to the extreme (2 drawings, 0.13 s) → **hold 0.4-0.6 s** → settle (0.3 s) | eyes and mouth stay small until the extreme, then pop (`wide`, `O`, ears `alarm`, Pip's puff-up) |
| Double take | glance and look away normally (0.5 s) → stop → snap back into a take | pairs naturally with G3 "last to notice" |
| Small take | the head stays still at frame centre while everything else moves; a tiny move reads big | for limited rig moves; keep faces in the central oval |
| Invisible anticipation | 1 drawing the opposite way before a snap | adds snap without being seen |
| Hard accent (bump, plop) | overshoots and bounces back, a 2-3 drawing recoil | soft accent (a wave) eases and keeps going |
| Impact | skip the contact drawing: show just before and the result after | Art Babbitt via Williams: show the result |
| Sound sync | the visual accent 1 drawing (2 frames) **before** the sound hit | Williams' rule of thumb for hitting a beat |
| Squash and stretch | keep the volume (`sq` up = narrower) | Milt Kahl: same amount of meat |
| Pause before the break | 0.3-0.6 s, almost still (a blink or breath only) | the "wait for it" |
| Hold the result | ≥1.0 s on the reaction face | our lesson 2 |

## 7. Sound for comedy
Give each break one accent: a pop, boing, plink or slide whistle up (never down onto someone's mistake). Rest the music
in the pause. Take sounds out of context the way Treg Brown did (a bottle squeak for a tuft popping up). Giggles are
little runs of notes, never voices. Everyone laughing together is a short warm chord plus giggle notes.

## 8. Comedy score anchors (shared with the blind reviewer)
Count only **landed gags**: the viewer saw the set-up, the break and a reaction. 9 = a landed gag every ≤15 s,
with a rule-of-three build and a topper, and reactions held. 8 = every ≤20 s, every set-up readable, at most one
weak gag. 7 = every ~20 s, but one key gag lands weakly (set-up unclear, too fast, or no reaction). 6 = fewer than one
per 20 s, or mostly charm. ≤5 = no landed gag.

## 9. Acting the face and body (research 9 Oct 2026, `docs/research-comedy-acting-oct-2026.md`)
For the animator (A = the rig must support it first: the A2 rig job) and the story-designer (D).
1. Eyes-only test: every emotion reads from eyes, lids, ears, head tilt and shoulders with the mouth hidden (Aardman's Shaun). A
2. Mood lives in the upper-lid angle: down-in = cross, down-out = sad. A
3. Blink only on a new thought, look or head turn: close 3-5 frames, brief hold, open 2-4 (teaching notes; test at 30 fps). A
4. Eye darts take 1-2 frames, lids follow the pupils, a big dart gets a blink; a dart shows "noticing" before the take. A
5. Moving hold: any hold over 0.5 s breathes (chest or shoulders) and drifts on from the last move; no frozen pose. A
6. Key expressions are asymmetric (one ear or lid different); eyes stay on one eyeline in a squash; mirror-check each take. A
7. One action verb per shot ("hides", "tests"); the verb changes only on a trigger the plan names (Ed Hooks). D
8. Write a gag as a body or face predicament first (stuck, tangled), then its cause; if it can't be drawn, drop it (Larva). D
9. Three sound points per gag: a cue before, silence during the action, a punctuation sound on the payoff (Tom & Jerry). D
10. Each character has a fixed noise vocabulary, one short non-speech sound per emotion, kept in the character bible (Pingu, Simon's Cat). D
Rig features for A2, ranked: eye states + pupil offset; upper-lid angle; ears with overlap; head tilt + breath loop; blink presets;
volume-keeping squash on an eyeline; 4-5 non-speech mouth shapes; sound-marker slots. Findings 3, 4 and 8 rest on search summaries only.
