## Point 1 in detail: the pumpkin and the cutting

- **What the sources say.** AAP HealthyChildren.org "Halloween Safety Tips" (page shows "Last Updated 9/29/2026"):
  let small children decorate, not carve, pumpkins; they can draw faces with markers while an adult does all the cutting;
  use a battery-operated flameless candle, a flashlight or a glow stick instead of a candle; and, if a real candle is
  ever used, do not put a candlelit pumpkin on a porch. CPSC (2023 Halloween release): leave carving to adults, and
  child helpers can scoop or trace the design with a marker; use battery lights or glow sticks, not open flame. A UK
  NHS trust page (Bradford District Care, 20 Oct 2023) keeps children away from knives, suggests painting a face as a
  no-carve alternative, and says an LED flameless candle is just as effective and much safer. UK fire and rescue
  services (Devon and Somerset, seen in a search summary, page not opened) say the same about battery tea lights.
  Nothing I found is outdated, and nothing is true only in one country (carving is a US/UK tradition; the lesson of
  children drawing, adults cutting and flameless light holds anywhere).
- **Is on-screen hand-cutting acceptable?** Yes. The shown behaviour is exactly the advised one: children draw, ask the
  grown-up (shot 22: the plan held up, a thumbs-up, then an open "wait there" palm), step back, and the grown-up
  cuts. No tool is drawn, no cutting motion is shown in detail, and a child cannot copy a tool they never saw. The
  fully off-screen alternative adds no safety, costs about 3 s and removes the transformation Short's "holes" step, so
  it is not needed. The only risk is a drawn blade or sawing slipping in during the build, which F1 closes.
- **The no-carve option.** The NHS trust page mentions painting a face and not carving at all. That would remove the
  cutting, but the projected smile (the wow) needs holes. The planned route (adult cuts, children draw) is the
  AAP-approved one and is kept.

## Point 2 in detail: the light

- **Battery tea light, no flame.** Correct and mainstream. The only gap was the button cell inside (F3).
- **Friends standing close.** Safe because there is no flame and no heat; this is exactly why AAP, CPSC and UK
  services recommend the battery light. The porch setting is fine only because of that, and the video never shows a
  flame. The tea light must read as an LED: white body, side switch, steady glow (F3).
- **Light on a body and a wing, never a face.** The plan's rule and numbers are sound (pattern on Crinkle's chest, on
  Pip's wing, on the wall; faces outside the beam). Two checks to make the rule numeric (carry-forward 2): from
  `rig2.py`, Crinkle's neck sits about 298 units above the floor (248 x 1.2), so his chin is near y 1182 and the 1.0x
  pattern (about 140 x 110 units, the face's own size) centred at y 1280 leaves only about 40 units of clearance at its
  top edge, and less when he looks down in shot 36. Pip's case is tighter: the 0.6x pattern is only about 84 units wide
  (x about 918-1002), and a wing of about 76 units reaching from x 820 (shot 40a) ends near x 896, short of it. To touch
  the light she must stand near x 850-870, where her beak (pointing at the pumpkin) comes within a few units of the
  pattern edge and past the plan's own x 880 face limit. This is not a plan fix (the plan lists this frame as
  prototype still 6), but it is the likeliest place for "light in a face" to happen. The prototype must show Pip's
  wing in the pattern with her face at least 30 units outside it; if it can't, Pip leans in with her head turned
  away (looking up at the wall face, which is natural), or the wing meets the pattern lower down where it is wider.
- **The wing-shaped gap in the giant face (shots 40a-40c).** Fine as a game, provided the gap is a soft, feathery
  dark patch crossing the smile for under 1.5 s each time, never a clawed or fingered shadow, with Pip visibly giggling
  at it.

## Point 3 in detail: fear of the dark, ages 2-6

- **What mainstream advice says.** Fear of the dark is normal at 2-6 because imagination grows faster than the ability
  to tell pretend from real (Zero to Three; AAP). Helpful: take the fear seriously, don't belittle, ridicule or tease,
  don't pressure the child to be brave, offer comfort, light, a comforting object or "protector", a sense of control
  and play (AAP fears page; Better Health Victoria; Zero to Three). AAP's Halloween advice for toddlers adds: toddlers
  need to know the scary stuff is pretend, and parents should watch for fears.
- **How the episode fits.** Pip's unease is shown as ordinary (puff-up, hiding behind a trusted friend, hugging
  herself). Crinkle worries then comforts at her level (crouch, pat). Nobody says she is silly. The fix is light plus
  company, not "be brave": a glowing friendly face the friends make together, with a part for Pip (she draws the heart
  nose), and no pressure (she chooses to reach into the light). The unease also isn't erased at once: she still
  glances at the dark wall after the glow, and the friends notice and respond, which models "listen to the feeling". There
  are no monsters, ghosts or skulls, and no peril. This is sound. F4 and F6 close the two places where the telling
  could slip (laughing, and wording).
- **What could frighten a 2-year-old, and the verdict.**

## Point 4 in detail: small parts, the heavy pumpkin, the chime

- **Markers.** The plan shows no caps (no choking object). Marker tips go only on the pumpkin; the tongue gags
  (shot 16 and 19) must never put the tip near or in the mouth (carry-forward 5).
- **Crinkle pushing a heavy pumpkin.** Acceptable: Crinkle is the big strong friend, the push is two slow quarter-turns
  on the spot (no lift, tip, wobble or drop), and nobody's feet are near it. The failed first push also shows "heavy
  things are hard", which is harmless here.
- **Ear bonk on the chime.** Acceptable once softened (F4). It is the established running joke (ears bonk things
  overhead; Character Bible), the hop is only about 35 units, and the chime is light. Nobody climbs.
